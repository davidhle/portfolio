In this individual research project I conducted, I explored the embedded
gender bias in popular voice assistant technologies. I designed cultural
probe booklets for female smartphone users to complete, in which they
explored their VA's reaction to certain prompts, reflected on them, and
speculated ideal responses and behaviors from the assistant.

> What are female smartphone users' perspectives on VAs, their responses
> to verbal abuse, and their utility in the future?
>
> — Research Question

## Project Context & Motivation

For a long time since its creation, Siri would respond to the prompt
"You're a bitch" with "I'd blush if I could."

Brought to light by UNESCO via their 2019 publication, [*I'd blush if I
could: closing gender divides in digital skills through
education*](https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000367416).

### Context of This HCI Project: Beyond Pink – Gender, Identity & Smartphones

This research was conducted as part of a larger HCI project course that
focused on applying advanced theories and methods to critical design
challenges. The course introduced us to queer, feminist, and intersectional
theories, alongside speculative and critical design approaches.

We were specifically tasked with using these various lenses to challenge
heteronormative assumptions and biases that had been embedded in
smartphone design and, in doing so, to push the boundaries of HCI
research.

### Harmful Gender Biases Enforced by Voice Assistants

Defaulted gender biases in Voice Assistants' (VA) has been prevalent since
their inception, as seen in their responses and reactions to certain
prompts. UNESCO brought this to light in their 2019 publication and detail
how design like this only further enforces negative stereotypes and
highlights the need to adjust VAs' design to prevent the permeation of
those stereotypes and their effects into society. I aimed to tackle this
in this semester research project through the perspectives of female
smartphone users themselves.

## Methodology

### Qualitative Research via the Cultural Probe Method

Especially since I myself do not identify as female, I chose methods for
this study that would lend themselves to gather rich and insightful data
that also would be flexible to the effort and time of students, as they
would be the participants I recruited.

The [cultural probe design
method](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/291224.291235) is one in which the
researcher creates an artifact package to give to participants in order to
evoke responses that are subjective, inspirational and fragmentary. In
doing so, the researcher can gain a deeper and empathetic understanding of
their participants through their own portrayal of their beliefs,
aesthetics and cultural concerns. This was important to me since I don't
have personal lived experience with harmful biases rooted on my gender
identity.

### Feminist HCI Framework

This study was primarily informed by two feminist HCI principles:

- **Ecology:** designing with the understanding that technology exists
  within and impacts the environments it takes space in (social, cultural,
  political)
- **Self-Disclosure:** the extent to which technology brings visibility to
  how its design affects its users as subjects

### The Probe's Design

The cultural probe I designed consisted of three main activities for
participants to complete:

- **Smartphone journal:** making note of how they use their smartphones
  throughout 3 days
- **VA Test:** testing provocative prompts to their VAs — while set to a
  "female voice" — and reflecting on the responses
- **Fill-in-the-story:** while taking on a role as a VA themself,
  participants speculate on how they would ideally react when their user
  activates them in scenarios of need, and speculate on their user's
  potential reactions to the help

### Pilot Study

To discover any improvements that could be made with the cultural probe's
booklet design (e.g., phrasing, visuals, prompts), I ran a pilot study.
There was one participant, and with their feedback, the following changes
were made:

- Increased journal duration
- Relocation of journaling reflection

The slideshow above depicts the final iteration of the booklet's design.

## Key Findings and Design Insights

### Authoritative & Brief vs. Empathetic and Thorough Responses

The participant responses throughout the activity book were diverse and
nuanced, and they centered around two main themes regarding the VAs'
response style: authoritative and brief, and empathetic and thorough.

**Authoritative & Brief**

Some participants had a preference for short and simple responses from the
VA rather than a longer explanatory one that probes further on why a user
said a certain provocative prompt.

**Empathetic & Thorough**

Other participants felt that their VA missed opportunities to explain,
educate, and/or console — these participants found it satisfactory when
the assistant provided resources, information, or emotional support.

### Design Implications

The most significant finding in this study was that though these two
themes prevailed amongst the participants' responses, even individual
participants were nuanced: one could have a preference of one response
style over the other, depending on the context of the interaction.

The results suggested that a further, more ethical and empathetic design
of VAs require a similarly nuanced design. In some contexts an
authoritative demeanor should be used, whereas others require more
empathy and softness — others may even need a mixture of both.

Thus, this study implicates the need for further research on VAs which
afford **customizability and a contextualized response-style system**.
