Dabble is a web app I designed and built to archive and visualize the
moments of my own dance practice, and the activities around it that still
shape my trajectory. It takes the spiral timeline I co-designed for my
master's thesis, [ChoreoMapper](/work/choreomapper), and turns the prototype
into a working tool: three linked visualizations, a data model that accepts
memories as imperfect as they really are, and space to revisit a moment
later and say how it looks now. It's pre-loaded with my own history, 40+
moments from 2009 to today.

> What value comes out of visualizing the moments of a practice that spans
> different styles, and activities beyond it, and how does their meaning
> change when I look back on them later?

## Project Context & Motivation

### From ChoreoMapper to Dabble

For my master's thesis, under the supervision of Eva Hornecker and Sarah
Fdili Alaoui, I conducted participatory research with female choreographers
and dance practitioners to co-design ChoreoMapper, an interactive digital
tool for dancers to trace their careers. It used moments in the dancers'
lives, both dance and non-dance related, as data points and visualized them
in a spiral.

The final artifact was a high-fidelity prototype, which meant the idea had
never been lived with. Dabble is my continuation: the same idea as a real
app that I use on my own data, and that anyone else can try.

### Personal Motivation

I'm a dancer, and my practice doesn't sit in one box. It has run from
C-walking in YouTube comment sections as a teenager, through shuffle dance,
house, pole, ballet, and yoga, alongside a research career in dance and HCI.
Existing trackers ask me to pick one activity and log sessions. What I
wanted was to see how the styles, people, places, and non-dance life events
sit around each other, and to find out what I'd learn from that about my own
trajectory.

### The Visualization Is Also the Input

Most personal-data tools separate the place where you enter data from the
place where you see it. Dabble treats them as one. You add, edit, and
reflect on entries from inside the visualizations themselves, so the picture
of your practice is also the way you write to it. This follows the idea of
*input visualization* (Bressa et al., CHI 2024): visualizations that support
the act of capturing data, not only reading it afterwards. It matters here
because the entries are memories, and a memory is easier to place when you
can see what's around it.

## Designing for Imperfect Memories

Logging a hobby after the fact rarely fits a neat form. A lot of the design
work went into a data model that accepts entries the way people remember
them.

### Flexible Date Precision

Entries don't need an exact date. Something can be logged as vaguely as
"October – November 2021," as a real date range for a multi-day event, or
without a time of day if only the day is remembered. The display text and
the underlying timestamps are kept separate, so a fuzzy memory can still be
placed on a timeline without pretending to be precise.

### Partial Locations

Locations are structured as country, city, and place, and every part is
optional. "Somewhere in Paris" is a valid answer, and so is "a club whose
name I've forgotten."

### Dynamic Categories

Categories aren't fixed in advance. When I pick up a new practice I can add
a category for it and it's woven into every visualization right away, with
its own color. Categories can be renamed or recolored later, and suggested
colors are spaced around the color wheel so a new category stays
distinguishable from the existing ones.

### Reflections

Any past entry can get dated, backdatable follow-up notes, each with its own
mood, text, and media. An entry keeps what I felt at the time; a reflection
captures how I feel about it now, and the two stay linked. Entries open in
small bookmark tabs in the sidebar, colored by category, with an
Original / Reflections switch inside each.

## Three Views of One Practice

The same entries can be explored through three visualizations, all driven by
one shared time-range brush, so narrowing to a window of time carries across
every view.

### Constellation

Entries appear as stars clustered by activity, echoing a night sky. Clicking
a star opens its entry; the view can be filtered by category or scrubbed
through time.

### Linear Timeline

A chronological view where multi-day events render as capsules and
overlapping periods are laid out in separate lanes so they stay legible.
Dragging moves the selected time window.

### Spiral Timeline

Time coils outward from a center point: the earliest entry at the core, the
most recent at the outer edge. This view comes directly from the ChoreoMapper
prototype. Year markers are small star glyphs; hovering one highlights that
year's band, and clicking zooms into it.

### A Journal, Not a Dashboard

Every view works in a dark night-sky palette and a warm, sketchbook-paper
light mode. The aim was something that feels closer to a journal you return
to than a dashboard you check, because the content is personal and the
reason to open it is reflection, not performance.

## Practice and Its Context

A dancer's trajectory isn't only dance. A research internship, a move to a
new city, a long stretch of poor health, or a change of studio all shape
what and how someone practices. Dabble gives these a place without letting
them crowd out the practice itself.

Each category belongs to one of two domains. **Movement** categories are the
practices themselves (shuffle, pole, yoga). **Orbit** categories are what
moves around them: dance research, health, and other life context. Movement
entries are drawn as solid marks; Orbit entries are hollow, and in the
constellation they gather into a soft aurora ribbon around the stars rather
than sitting among them.

The two domains read as different kinds of thing at a glance, but they're
still on the same timeline, so I can see, for example, how a stretch of
reduced practice lines up with what was going on around it.

## Turning Points

The final design is the result of a sequence of decisions, many made after
using the app on my own history and finding something wrong. These are the
ones that changed it most.

### 💡 The data model grew through use

The first version asked for a title, date, category, and notes. Entering my
own history exposed what it couldn't hold: events I only remembered as a
season, trips that spanned days, places I could name only as a city, moods
that only made sense in hindsight. Each of those became a field
(`dateDisplay`, end dates, partial locations, mood, reflections). The model
was shaped by trying to put a real past into it.

### 💡 Dim, don't hide

When filtering by category, the first instinct was to remove everything that
didn't match. But removing entries removes context: you can't see what else
was happening. Non-matching entries now fade back instead of disappearing,
so a filter reads as "look here" rather than "this is all there is."

### 💡 The canvas never resizes

Opening the sidebar first shrank the visualization to make room. Every open
and close re-laid out the view, and the thing I was looking at moved. I
changed it so the visualization is full-bleed and the sidebar overlays it,
with a resizable width and a switchable side. Nothing shifts when you read
an entry.

### 💡 Drawing the spiral honestly

My first spiral drew duration bands as fixed-radius arcs. They looked right
at a glance and were wrong in the details: bands drifted off the curve and
didn't line up with the dates they claimed to cover. I rebuilt them by
sampling points along the real spiral, so a band's start and end sit on the
exact stretch of time it represents. Lanes for overlapping events are
assigned longest-first with no gaps, so stacked bands read as one
continuous structure.

### 💡 From year labels to landmarks

Text year labels cluttered the spiral and fought with the entries. Replacing
them with small star glyphs made them landmarks instead of text: quiet until
hovered, then they light up their band, and a click zooms into that year.

### 💡 Opacity was already taken

Opacity already means "filtered out," so it couldn't also mean "Orbit."
Orbit needed to differ by shape, not by fade. That led to the solid / hollow
distinction, and on the constellation to the aurora ribbon, built with SVG
turbulence and displacement rather than WebGL. I'd tried a shader-based
aurora, and it was beautiful but heavy and hard to keep consistent with the
rest of the SVG scene and its light and dark modes.

### 💡 Reflections as sticky notes

I considered letting people edit an entry to update how they felt. That
overwrites the very thing the app is for: what I thought *then*. Reflections
are separate, dated notes attached to an entry, like a sticky note on an old
journal page. The original stays intact and the change in perspective
becomes visible.

### 💡 Two ways to reset

Escape and the on-screen reset button do different things on purpose.
Escape peels back one layer at a time (deselect, then close, then clear the
view), so it's safe to hit by habit. The reset button goes straight back to
the full view, for when I'm lost. A confirmation toast says what changed.

### What I dropped

- Clustering Orbit entries by domain in the constellation: it made the
  aurora feel like another category rather than a different kind of thing,
  so I reverted it.
- A form-based multi-step add flow: pressing Next submitted the entry. I
  replaced the form with plain controlled inputs.

## Working with AI

I built Dabble in React, TypeScript, and D3, using Claude Code to implement
and a separate Claude conversation to plan, reason through design decisions,
and write the prompts. I held the design direction and tested every change
against my own data. A recurring pattern was that a change looked fine in
isolation and was wrong when applied to my real history, which is what
surfaced most of the turning points above.

## Try It Yourself

### Bring Your Own Data

Dabble runs entirely in the browser. Anyone visiting can reset it to a blank
slate and start their own constellation from their own practices, like a
digital journal for tracking progress and moments. Entries can be exported as
a portable JSON file and imported again later.

## Future Work

Dabble is a working tool and also a first step. Right now it holds one
person's history.

### Limits so far

- **One person, one dataset.** Everything I've learned about it comes from my
  own use, so I can't say yet what holds up for other people or other
  practices.
- **Data lives in the browser.** Export and import is the only way to move or
  back up data.
- **Reflection is manual.** The app makes it possible to revisit a moment,
  but doesn't help me notice which moments are worth revisiting.

### What's next

- **Connections between entries**: visualizing links across events, such as
  shared locations, feelings, or life events
- **Material artifacts**: objects tied to a practice or era rather than a
  single date (a pair of shoes, a song, a video that shaped a style), linked
  to the moments they relate to
- **Multi-category entries**: e.g. a trip that involved both shuffle dancing
  and pole training, possibly shown as a blend of each category's color
- **Richer media and locations**: photo and voice-note attachments, previews
  of linked videos, and mappable locations
- **A list/table view** for browsing entries outside the visualizations
- **Accounts and cross-device sync**, replacing export/import as the main way
  to save data

### Longitudinal Research Directions

Because reflections are dated separately from the moments they refer to,
Dabble records something most trackers don't: *how my account of the same
event changes over time*. That makes it a natural instrument for longitudinal
research.

**Questions I'd like to explore**

- When people revisit the same moment months or years later, what changes:
  the facts, the emotions, or the meaning they give it?
- Does seeing a practice laid out over time change how people understand
  their own trajectory, and does it change what they do next?
- Do the kinds of moments people record shift over a practice's life, from
  firsts and milestones, to community, to setbacks and doubts?
- How do life events outside the practice relate to changes inside it, and
  what do people notice only after seeing both on one timeline?

**A possible path**

1. *Long-term self-study.* Continue using Dabble on my own practice as an
   autobiographical design study, with scheduled revisits of earlier entries,
   and treat the accumulating reflections as data about how hindsight works.
2. *Diary study with other dancers.* Invite a small group of dancers from
   different styles to keep their own Dabble over several months, with
   periodic interviews about what they recorded, what they went back to, and
   what the views surfaced for them.
3. *Design iteration from what's learned.* Use both to decide which of the
   planned features (connections, artifacts, multi-category entries) earn
   their place.

**Considerations**

The entries are personal and sometimes about health, relationships, or
community conflict. Dabble currently stores nothing outside the user's
browser, and that is a feature for this kind of work. Any study would keep it
that way by default: participants decide what to export and share, and
consent covers both what is collected and how it will be represented.
