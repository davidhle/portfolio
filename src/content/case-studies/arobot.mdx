ARobot is a speculative concept for an augmented-reality robot family,
designed with two teammates, Mira Thieme and Lily Hecke, during a Design
Thinking: Ideation course I took as an Erasmus exchange student at TU
Wien. The brief asked us to design a digital artifact for Seestadt Aspern,
one of Vienna's ongoing smart-city districts, and we chose to explore
sustainable everyday mobility — car use versus walking, cycling, and
public transit — as the behavior worth designing around.

## Project Context

Seestadt Aspern is one of Vienna's largest urban-development districts, a
smart city still in progress. Our first step was simply getting to know
the neighborhood on foot, which grounded the rest of the project: we
wanted to encourage discussions about different ways of getting around,
and to reflect on our own mobility habits as a starting point. That
became the project's core goal — supporting environmentally-conscious
behavior in people's everyday movement through the district.

## Initial Concept & Storyboard

Our first concept was a small robot stationed near a bike rack, viewed
through something like a pair of binoculars. Looking through it, a
resident would see whether the robot's "community" was growing or
shrinking, depending on how much the neighborhood around it relied on
cars versus other ways of getting around. We sketched a storyboard to
walk through this interaction, from first noticing the robot to
understanding what it was telling you.

## Expert Interview

To stress-test the AR side of the concept, I interviewed Christian
Schönauer, a postdoc researcher in TU Wien's AR/VR group. His key
insight: head-mounted AR displays are heavy and impractical for casual,
everyday public use — an LED or screen-based display built into the
environment itself would work much better for something people encounter
briefly in passing. That pushed the concept away from a personal viewer
and toward a stationary public installation.

I ran a design workshop with my teammate, facilitated by our instructor,
to work through what that stationary installation should actually look
and feel like. We landed on a large, stationary screen with an
anthropomorphic form — big eyes, a simple smiling face, an antenna —
whose glow reflects the neighborhood's collective mood around sustainable
mobility. That mood is driven by a voluntary, game-like system residents
opt into, tracking sustainable mobility choices across the district.

whiteboard iteration sketched out with a teammate, and finally a clean

## Placement & the Interaction Model

We imagined robots placed at several points across Seestadt — metro and
bike stations, playgrounds, and other well-liked public spots — turning
the neighborhood itself into a shared board: residents' everyday trips
double as moves in a lightweight, voluntary game, tracked through travel,
path, and special cards.

Each robot's interaction zone is deliberately shielded and contained: a
sensor projects an AR field of view onto a small area nearby, revealing a
"family" of smaller robot characters whose well-being visibly responds to
how sustainably the neighborhood around them is getting around.

## Looking Ahead

Looking beyond the course project, we imagined tying the game into
Seestadt's existing resident smart-card system (the real "Seestadt Card")
as the way residents would opt in, and scaling the concept across
Seestadt's different neighborhood zones rather than a single
installation.

## A Note on Process

For the final presentation, I designed a narrative poster that traced
each design method we used along the actual metro line route into
Seestadt — a small piece of process documentation that ended up
structuring how we presented the whole project to a non-expert audience.
